# The Cotswolds

We spent a week in the [Cotswolds][cotswolds], that stretch of limestone
hills and villages across Gloucestershire and its neighboring counties. The
name comes from *cod*, an old word for sheep enclosure, and *wold*, meaning
rolling upland. Sheep built this place. Wool money built the churches. The
stone did the rest.

The area covers about 800 square miles of Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty,
a designation the English take seriously. You notice it immediately: dry
stone walls, hedgerows, and villages that look as if someone color-matched
them to a jar of honey.

# Honey Stone

The look comes from oolitic limestone, a Jurassic rock full of tiny spherical
grains. In the sun it reads gold. On a gray afternoon it reads warm anyway.
Builders have used it here for centuries, so a town of 500 people can look
as if it arrived all at once, in the same century, from the same quarry.

I kept expecting a film crew. Nobody showed up with clapperboards. The
place just looks like that.

# Villages

## Bourton-on-the-Water

[Bourton-on-the-Water][bourton] gets called the Venice of the Cotswolds,
which oversells both Venice and Bourton. The River Windrush runs through
the center, low and clear, with low stone bridges you can cross in a few
steps. Ducks have better real estate than some of the shops.

Crowds come for the model village, the motor museum, and the chance to
stand on a bridge and take the same photograph everyone else took. We
walked the river, bought a coffee, and left before the afternoon coach
tours filled the High Street.

## Bibury

William Morris called [Bibury][bibury] the most beautiful village in
England. [Arlington Row][arlington], a line of 14th-century weavers'
cottages, still does the work of proving him right. The cottages face a
water meadow. People photograph them from the road, then from the other
side of the road, then from a slightly different angle on the road.

We did the same. I will not pretend otherwise.

## Stow-on-the-Wold

[Stow-on-the-Wold][stow] sits at about 800 feet, a market town at the
junction of several old roads. The market square has stocks. The church
of St. Edward has a north door flanked by ancient yew trees, the kind of
image that ends up on calendars and in fantasy films.

We walked the square, looked at the door, and found a pub. That sequence
held up for most of the week.

# Public Footpaths

England's public footpaths remain one of my favorite systems anywhere.
A marked path can cut across a pasture, along a stream, past someone's
garden wall, and out onto a lane, and you have every right to walk it.
The Cotswold Way runs 102 miles from Chipping Campden to Bath. We did
not do 102 miles. We did enough to learn that sheep do not care about
your itinerary, and that stiles exist to humble people who sit at desks.

The walking is the point. Villages sit close enough that a morning on
foot delivers a different church, a different pub, and another row of
that honey stone.

# Narrow Lanes

Driving between villages means single-track roads with hedgerows for
walls and the occasional oncoming car as a negotiation. Pull-outs exist.
Courtesy exists. Speed does not. After a year of travel, this felt like
the correct pace: you cannot rush a lane that is one car wide and lined
with stone.

The GPS remained optimistic. The lanes remained lanes.

# Pubs

A week in the Cotswolds is a week of pubs. Stone floors, low beams, a
fireplace that has opinions, and a menu that assumes you walked here.
I remain a coffee person, but I will not refuse a pint after a few miles
of footpath. Sunday roast showed up when it should. So did sticky toffee
pudding.

If you come for the villages, stay for the walking. If you come for the
walking, the pubs will find you.
